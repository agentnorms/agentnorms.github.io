Foundations for Agentic Risk, Actuarial Science, and Insurance

AgentNorm studies the foundations of decisions under uncertainty. This programme, developed through Shuo Li Liu’s research, connects evidence about agentic systems to actuarial loss models, insurance prices, and the financing of shared safety.

From evaluation to insurance

AI evaluations provide evidence about a system in a particular setting. Insurance requires a further account of what can be lost, which losses a contract covers, and how claims may arrive together. A shared model, update, tool, or service can expose many users to the same failure.

Decision theory under uncertainty supplies a foundation for this transition: state the admissible loss models, the evidence supporting them, and the preferences or constraints governing a decision. Knightian uncertainty enters when the evidence supports several probability models without identifying one trusted law.

Agentic risk
Which failures can arise across actions, environments, and shared dependencies? What connects evaluation evidence to deployment?
Actuarial science
How do those failures translate into covered loss frequency, severity, dependence, and aggregate exposure?
Insurance economics
How do premiums and contract terms affect participation, prevention incentives, and the funding of shared safety?

This programme connects those questions through explicit assumptions. Its current insurance paper develops a theoretical model of dependence, pricing, and collective funding.

The AI insurance paper

Research draft · version 12 · 1 November 2026

Tail-Risk Pricing and Collective AI Safety: Insurance under Dependence Ambiguity

Can greater concern about catastrophic AI losses weaken the insurance incentives for collective safety? The paper holds individual loss frequencies fixed and varies dependence, the uncertainty surrounding it, and the insurer’s treatment of tail losses.

In the benchmark, losses either diversify across a large pool or arise through a common regime. Its probability is known only to lie within an interval. Insurers price a bounded liability using a worst-case mean–expected-shortfall criterion; customers decide whether coverage is worth its price.

Main results

01 / Demand

A larger saving per policy can generate less total value.

Putting more weight on a fixed tail statistic increases the premium saving from a control on each insured exposure. It also makes insurance more expensive. If coverage contracts enough, the control’s total insurance-market value falls. The continuous-demand model allows this reversal while both the original and improved markets remain active.

Propositions 5–6 · §§5.1–5.2

02 / Tail focus

Extreme-tail pricing can stop distinguishing a risk reduction.

Increasing the expected-shortfall confidence level changes which losses the price criterion distinguishes. For bounded loss models with a common mean, if a control lowers robust expected shortfall but leaves the same largest admitted payout possible, its market value tends to zero as the assessed tail becomes extreme. Under the stated demand condition, insurance remains active throughout.

Proposition 7 · §5.4

03 / Investment

Optimal safety effort can rise, then drop to zero.

In the common-regime model, suppose every finite effort leaves positive residual common risk and effort costs grow without bound. Sufficiently extreme tail focus then makes zero effort optimal for collective insurance-market surplus. With exponential risk reduction and linear cost, optimal positive effort rises as the tail narrows, then is abandoned when cost exhausts its gain.

Eliminating common risk at a sufficiently low finite cost is a countercase. The result concerns the insurance channel; it does not value uninsured harm or establish a decentralized investment equilibrium.

Propositions 8–9 · §5.6

04 / Funding

A valuable control need not fund itself.

With publicly known benefits, sequential commitments and credible cancellation can fund a beneficial intervention. With private benefits, the paper derives an exact funding bound under dominant-strategy truthfulness, voluntary participation relative to no project, and efficient project selection at every type profile.

Bundling insurance and project payments does not remove that bound if both allocations must remain efficient. A self-financing unanimity rule is possible, but can reject projects with positive aggregate gains.

Propositions 3, 10–12 · §§4, 6

What the results establish

The analysis separates risk reduction, generated market value, its distribution, and the amount a mechanism can collect. These quantities need not move together. Wider dependence ambiguity can also reduce the value of a fixed physical intervention without changing the true initial risk.

The model prices a specified, bounded insured layer. Its premiums compensate stipulated risk-bearing preferences; they are not actuarially fair expected-loss prices or statutory capital requirements. Bounded coverage does not imply bounded total harm. The results do not measure the total social value of AI safety or establish a general case against prudent underwriting.

Appendices examine finite pools, provider pricing, and the distribution of gains. In particular, taking a large-pool limit before an extreme-tail limit can change the answer. Proofs are accompanied by numerical checks described in the manuscript; those checks are not empirical calibration.

Research programme

The next questions concern the evidence and institutions needed to use this foundation in practice:

  • Evidence and admissible models. Determine when evaluation results justify narrowing a loss-model set, and which deployment assumptions remain necessary.
  • Shared exposure. Map model versions, tools, and common services to joint insured losses. Group observations by failure episode rather than treating every affected user as an independent event.
  • Contracts and incentives. Study how limits, exclusions, monitoring, and verifiable controls affect insurance demand and the financing of prevention.
  • Preference alignment. Examine whose losses and preferences a risk criterion represents, and how collective decisions account for heterogeneous interests.

These are research directions. Estimating premiums or control effectiveness requires contract definitions, exposure data, verifiable interventions, and evidence about willingness to pay.
